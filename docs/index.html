Project Name

Blue Marble Records

High-Level Project Summary

Blue Marble Records is a web app that turns NASA Earth Information Center (EIC) frames into music, made for blind and low-vision people. The visitor picks one of 30 places on Earth. A needle reads that point of the NASA frame, decoded back into its physical value, and the browser composes music from the values live, date after date, like a record turning. It has two records: sea surface temperature anomaly (NASA SVS 5176) and active fires seen by VIIRS (NASA SVS 5113).

It answers The Earth Information Jukebox by pairing EIC frames with sonification generated in real time from the picture itself: we read each number back from the frame's own pixels, and we checked that decoding against a separate NASA rendering (NASA GIBS). Every control works from the keyboard, and every value is also a sentence for a screen reader.

It matters because Earth data is published mostly as pictures. Here a person who cannot see the map can follow, by ear, how the sea warmed or cooled at one place and when fire came and went.

Detailed Project Description

What it does

The page opens on one question, "How do you want to start?", and three choices: a guided tour with the visitor's own screen reader, a guided tour with the app's voice, or "Explore on my own". The interface comes in English and Brazilian Portuguese.

The guided tour visits four places: the North Atlantic south of Greenland and the equatorial Pacific on the sea record, then Uberlândia (Brazil) and the miombo woodlands of Angola on the fire record, playing each record from start to end.

Exploring freely, the visitor chooses a record and one of its saved places (17 on the ocean, 13 on land), and can:

  • Play through time: the values of that place become music, one date after another.
  • Compare: hear the same place on two dates together. One note means little change; two notes beating against each other mean it changed.
  • Hear the sound legend: low, middle, high, and the crackle that means no data.
  • Ask in words: keys that say the value under the needle, the place with its latitude and longitude, and the highest and lowest places on that date.
  • Open "Data and method": the source, scale, validation numbers and method of each record.

For sighted visitors the same state is drawn as a picture: the Earth in space with the NASA frame of the date laid over it, and a vinyl record around it whose grooves are the dates.

How it works

  1. Decoding, done ahead of time in Python. EIC frames are pictures, not data files. On the sea record, each pixel takes the value of the nearest color of the colorbar NASA publishes with the visualization, measured in the CIELAB color space; pixels far from every colorbar color (land, sea ice, coasts) are "no data". On the fire record, a pixel is fire when it differs clearly from the same pixel of an empty frame, the base map. Both are reduced to a 1 degree grid, one small image per date.
  2. Reading, in the browser. The needle reads the cell that contains the chosen place and turns the pixel back into a value on the record's fixed scale.
  3. Music, composed live. Nothing is a recorded song. The only recordings are single notes and single gestures of real instruments: our own guitar and CC0 sample libraries. Every chord, pattern and melody is decided while it plays, by rules we wrote. On the sea record the distance from normal sets the tension, warmer water sounds bright and colder water dark, and each date lasts one bar; when the temperature moves by three quarters of a degree or more, a harp runs up (warmer) or down (colder). On the fire record, more fire activity means more tension and a heavier, louder band, and the harp runs up for more fire, down for less. The place sets the key and the tempo, and the same data always gives the same music.
  4. Words. Each reading is also one short sentence (place, date, value, unit), built from a template and sent to a single live region for the visitor's own screen reader. No language model runs in the app, and there is no backend.

Built for the ear

  • Every control is a native HTML control. The 3D picture is hidden from assistive technology, and every choice it offers is also a button, a list or a slider.
  • One key map holds everywhere: up and down change the place, left and right switch between sea and fire, Space or Enter plays and pauses, Esc or S stops all sound. A keyboard guide lists every key.
  • Nothing sounds until the visitor acts, and the music lowers while a sentence is announced.
  • No data is never silence and never a low note: it is a soft vinyl crackle, the "dead groove".
  • The optional voice guide is a synthetic voice, labelled as one. It never reads the values and never speaks over the music.
  • On Android phones that can vibrate, the value is also given as 1 to 5 pulses, and no data as a rough buzz.

Benefits

  • A person who cannot see the map has a way to follow one place through time, by ear and in words, without asking anyone to describe a picture.
  • The mapping is fixed and written down, so a listener can learn it once and compare places and dates.
  • It says what the data cannot say: a value at the end of a scale is announced as such, and fire is given as fire activity (hot-pixel density), a relative number.
  • It runs in a browser, with no install and no account.

What we hope to achieve

We hope to put more EIC records under the same needle, to hold listening sessions with blind and low-vision people and publish what we learn, and to show that a picture of Earth data can be published with a sound and a sentence that carry the same number.

Tools, languages and software

  • HTML, CSS and JavaScript (ES modules): no framework, no build step, no backend.
  • Tone.js for sound (Web Audio) and three.js for the globe (WebGL).
  • Python with NumPy, Pillow and OpenEXR to decode and validate the NASA frames.
  • An acoustic guitar recorded by the team during the hackathon, CC0 sample libraries for the other instruments, and ElevenLabs text to speech for the optional voice guide.
  • nginx in a Docker container to serve the app; Git and GitHub.
  • AI coding assistants (large language models): see Use of Super Intelligence (SI).

What is not SI-generated

  • The music. Recorded notes of real instruments, composed live in the browser from the data by rules the team wrote. No file among the recorded notes was made by a generative model. The reference sounds (the needle drop, the crackle of no data, single tones) are synthesized in the browser by our own code.
  • The images. NASA frames and NASA imagery, credited under References. The record, the icons and the opening animation are drawn in code. No image was made with an image generator.
  • The data values. Every value is computed from a NASA frame by the pipeline. None was written by hand or produced by a model.
  • The app while it runs. No language model runs in the app. Its sentences are templates filled with the decoded values.

The decisions about the concept, the mapping from data to sound and what ships were made by the team. Nothing we generated uses, contains or modifies NASA logos, flags or mission identifiers.

NASA data is the core of the project: the music is composed from values decoded from NASA Earth Information Center frames.

How a value is decoded Nearest colorbar color in CIELAB, then the median of the valid pixels of each 1 degree cell. A cell with fewer than half valid pixels is no data. Pixels that differ from the empty base map are fire, then fire pixels over land pixels in each 1 degree cell. A cell that is less than half land is no data.

How we use the frames as data

The challenge's resources link the EIC gallery, whose Daily Visualizations page lists both visualizations. We took the unlabeled equirectangular frames (4096 by 2048 pixels) for the 10th of each month and decoded them with the Python scripts in pipeline/. The browser does not read NASA's servers: it loads the small files that decoding produced (web/data/), whose format is described in docs/DATA-FORMAT.md.

How we checked the decoding

The frames are pictures, so we checked our reading of them against NASA GIBS, a separate NASA rendering of the same data. This tests our decoding, not the satellite data. GIBS is used only for this check, never as a source of the values the app plays.

Every compared point is published as validation.csv next to each record's data, the same numbers are shown in the app under "Data and method", and the full report is docs/VALIDATION.md.

What the numbers can and cannot say

  • Top of scale. The sea scale ends at −4 and +4 °C. A value at the top is given as "at or above +4 °C (top of scale)", never as a measured number.
  • Fire is fire activity (hot-pixel density). The value is the share of land pixels covered by detection dots in the NASA rendering. It is relative: not a measure of fire power, and not the share of land that burned.
  • Snapshots. Each sea date is one daily frame, not a monthly mean, and each fire date is one 5-day window. On the fire record, 10 November 2026 is left out because the newest frames were still being filled in.
  • El Niño and La Niña are never read from the pixels. They are cited from the NOAA Climate Prediction Center.
  • No data has its own sound and its own words, so a missing value is not mistaken for a low one.

Two comparisons the app suggests

  • Equatorial Pacific (near 0°, 140°W), sea record: −0.66 °C on 10 November 2025, and at or above +4 °C (top of scale) on 10 November 2026. The NOAA Climate Prediction Center issued a La Niña Advisory on 13 November 2025 and an El Niño Advisory on 12 November 2026.
  • Uberlândia, Brazil, fire record: fire dots on 1.2% of land pixels on 10 February 2025 (wet season) and on 22.0% on 10 September 2025 (dry season).

How to run it

With Docker, from the root of the repository:

docker build -t blue-marble-records .
docker run -p 8080:80 blue-marble-records

Then open http://localhost:8080/ in a browser. Without Docker, serve the web/ folder with any static file server. There is no backend and no build step.
